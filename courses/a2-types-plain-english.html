The types, in plain English: four ways to guess what you want

There are only a handful of core ideas behind every "recommended for you" you have ever seen, and you can understand all of them with zero math. This session walks through the four families - people like you, items bought together, more like what you already like, and simply what is trending - each with an everyday analogy, and pins down the one distinction that trips everyone up: the difference between "people like you" and "items like this".

Part 1 · the two lenses on the same data

"People like you" vs "items like this" 7 min live

The first two families are both called collaborative filtering because they both learn purely from behaviour - who bought or watched what - with no idea what the items actually are. They are two lenses on the exact same data, and the difference is simply what you compare.

LiveCustomer-to-customer (user-user), in one analogy2 min▶

"People like you also liked." The system finds shoppers whose history looks like yours - you and Ann both bought the same five things - then recommends what those look-alikes liked that you have not tried yet. It is a friend with similar taste saying "if you liked those, you'll love this".

  • The signal it uses: your behaviour compared to other people's behaviour.
  • Where it shines: taste discovery - it surfaces things you would never search for.
  • Where it struggles: brand-new users (no history to match) and huge shopper bases (comparing everyone to everyone is expensive).
LiveItem-to-item, and why Amazon flipped the lens3 min▶

"Bought / watched together." Instead of finding people like you, item-item finds items like the one you are looking at - things that keep showing up in the same baskets. You add a yoga mat; it suggests the blocks and bottle that so often ride along.

  • The signal it uses: which items co-occur in people's behaviour - again, no idea what the items are.
  • Why the flip matters: there are usually far fewer items than shoppers, and item relationships are stable, so you can compute them ahead of time. That is what let it scale.
Real world

Amazon's item-to-item is the canonical example. Linden, Smith & York published it in 2003 (IEEE Internet Computing 7(1):76-80), and it won IEEE's "Test of Time" award in 2017 for how durable the idea proved. It is the engine behind "customers who bought this also bought". The leader insight: Amazon chose item-item precisely because comparing millions of shoppers to each other did not scale, but comparing a smaller, stable set of items did. The choice of lens was an engineering-and-business decision, not just a math one.

The one distinction to remember Both are collaborative filtering; both learn only from behaviour. User-user asks "who is similar to this person?" and borrows their taste. Item-item asks "what is similar to this thing?" and suggests the companions. Same data, opposite anchor.
Part 2 · reading the item itself

Content-based: "more like the things you like" 5 min live

The third family ignores other people entirely. It reads the features of the items you already liked - the tags, the genre, the brand, the description - builds a profile of your taste, and finds other items whose features match.

LiveThe analogy, and its blind spot2 min▶

"You loved calm nature documentaries, here is another calm nature documentary." It is a librarian who knows the tags on every book and keeps handing you the same shelf.

  • The signal it uses: the item's own features - no other shoppers needed.
  • Where it shines: brand-new items (a fresh release already has tags, so it can be recommended on day one - the thing collaborative filtering cannot do), and easy explanations ("because you like nature").
  • Where it struggles: it can get too narrow - only ever more of the same, a self-made filter bubble. It also depends on having good tags in the first place.
Part 3 · the baseline and the blend

Popularity, hybrid, and which family when 5 min live

The fourth "family" is the honest baseline: popularity - just show what is trending. It ignores you completely, which is its weakness and, for a stranger with no history, its strength. And in the real world, the best products blend several families into a hybrid.

LivePopularity, and the one-line nod to hybrid & deep2 min▶
  • Popularity ("what's trending"): everyone sees the same list. It ignores you - which is exactly why it is the safe fallback for a brand-new visitor, and the honest baseline every personalized model has to beat.
  • Hybrid: the families are not rivals; production systems blend them - popularity for the first visit, item-item on a product page, content-based for fresh items, collaborative once there is history.
  • Deep / modern: the giants layer neural models (like Alibaba's DIN across the Lazada family) on top to weigh many signals at once. It is more of the same idea with far more horsepower - and far more engineering. We touch it lightly in session 6.
The leader question to ask When a team proposes a family, the useful question is not "is it the fanciest?" but "does it fit the data and the goal?" - do we have the behaviour for collaborative, the tags for content-based, or neither yet (so popularity)? That one question is most of the decision.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 20-30 min total

  • Pick three recommenders you use (a shop, a streaming app, a feed). Classify each into a family: user-user, item-item, content-based, or popularity.
  • For each, name the signal it is clearly using - other people's behaviour, item co-occurrence, item features/tags, or nothing about you at all.
  • Find one example of item-item in the wild ("customers also bought", "frequently bought together") and one of content-based ("because you watched", "more like this").
  • Spot a case where a product is obviously blending families - for example popularity on the first screen but personalized once you have a history.
  • Optional: skim the abstract of the Amazon 2003 item-to-item paper and note the reason they give for choosing items over users.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What is the difference between user-user and item-item filtering?

Same behaviour data, opposite anchor. User-user borrows the taste of look-alike people; item-item suggests the companions of the item in hand.

2 · A brand-new film with no viewers yet can still be recommended by which family?

Content-based matches item features to your taste, so a fresh item with tags can be surfaced on day one. That is its edge over collaborative filtering on new items.

3 · Why did Amazon choose item-item over comparing shoppers to each other?

Comparing millions of shoppers to each other did not scale; comparing a smaller, stable set of items did. The 2003 paper won IEEE's Test-of-Time award for that durable insight.
