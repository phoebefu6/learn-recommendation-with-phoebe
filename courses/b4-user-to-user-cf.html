Part 0

The most natural recommender

If a friend with near-identical taste raves about a book, you will probably like it too. Customer-to-customer collaborative filtering is that instinct written as math: find the users most similar to you by their purchase history, then recommend the items they bought that you have not seen. It needs no item features and no tags - only the interactions matrix from b1. Tonight we build the whole thing on Kirana, and we meet its two famous weaknesses head on, because those weaknesses are precisely why item-to-item (which you saw in b3) took over at Amazon-scale. This is the same math the "User-user CF" button runs in the b3 live recommender - build it here, then go watch it there.

Part 1 · covers the recsys textbook (neighbourhood CF)

Find neighbours, then borrow their extra items 6 min live

User-user CF is two moves. First, measure how similar every other shopper is to the target. Second, take the items those similar shoppers bought that the target has not, and rank them. The items your neighbours have that you lack become your recommendations.

LiveCosine similarity (and a word on Pearson)3 min▶

To rank neighbours you need a number for "how alike are these two users?". Treat each user's row in the matrix as a vector and measure the angle between them:

  • Cosine similarity - the cosine of the angle between two users' item vectors. 1 = identical direction (same taste), 0 = no overlap. It ignores how much someone bought and focuses on the pattern, which is why it is the default for implicit data like Kirana's.
  • Pearson correlation - cosine after subtracting each user's mean. It shines on explicit ratings, where it cancels out that some users rate generously and others harshly. For binary purchase data, cosine is the natural choice.
  • Weighted vote - a candidate item's score is the sum of the similarities of the neighbours who bought it. More neighbours, and closer ones, push an item up.
Real world

This is the "customers like you also bought" line in its original form. Early collaborative filtering systems computed exactly this - find your neighbourhood, aggregate their tastes. It is wonderfully explainable: you can literally say "we suggest this because shoppers with baskets like yours bought it". The catch is what happens when "shoppers like you" becomes tens of millions of rows.

Self-studyWhy Amazon flipped from user-user to item-item2 min read▶

User-user CF is the intuitive starting point, but it has two structural problems at scale - and the contrast with item-item (b3) is the whole lesson:

  • It scales worse. Users vastly outnumber items and change constantly - every new purchase shifts a user's vector, so the user-user similarity matrix goes stale fast and is expensive to recompute. Item-item similarities are far more stable: relationships between products barely move day to day, so you can precompute them offline.
  • It is more cold-start-fragile. A brand-new shopper with one purchase has almost no reliable neighbours, so recommendations are weak until they build history. Item-item sidesteps this: given even one item the user just viewed, you can serve "items like this" immediately.

This is precisely the argument Linden, Smith & York made in Amazon.com Recommendations: Item-to-Item Collaborative Filtering (IEEE Internet Computing, 2003). Amazon moved the heavy computation to the item-item matrix so recommendations stayed fast and fresh for hundreds of millions of users. Same collaborative-filtering idea, transposed - and it won IEEE's Test-of-Time award in 2017.

So why learn user-user at all? Because it is the clearest mental model of collaborative filtering, it is genuinely useful when users far under-number items (or update rarely), and understanding its failure modes is what makes the item-item choice in b3 click. Build it, feel its limits, then know when to reach for its transpose.
Part 2 · covers neighbourhood-based prediction

From similarity to a ranked list 4 min live

The recommender is a three-stage flow. Similarity feeds a weighted score for every candidate item; the scores feed a ranked top-N of things the user has not seen.

Livek-nearest-neighbour prediction, in words2 min▶

You rarely use all other users - just the k nearest (say the 20 most similar). For each item the target has not bought, sum the similarities of the neighbours (among those k) who did buy it. That sum is the item's score; sort descending and take the top-N. It is a weighted vote where closer neighbours count for more - the k-nearest-neighbour idea applied to users.

In production you would not hand-roll this: surprise gives you tuned KNN CF with baselines, and implicit gives you fast neighbourhood and matrix models for implicit data. We build it by hand first so the library is never a black box.

The user-user similarity matrix ★ 11 min · everyone builds

Build the similarity matrix with scikit-learn and find any shopper's nearest neighbours. We reuse Kirana's mat from b1: rows are users, columns are items, 1 = bought.

Rebuild Kirana's users x items matrix (same shape as b1's mat).

Compute pairwise cosine similarity across users with cosine_similarity.

Wrap it in a labelled DataFrame so you can look up any user's neighbours by name.

Print a target user's nearest neighbours, most similar first.

★ Run it now (Python) - user-user cosine similarityimport pandas as pd from sklearn.metrics.pairwise import cosine_similarity # Kirana users x items matrix (implicit, 1 = bought) - as built in b1 tx = pd.DataFrame({ "user": ["u1","u1","u1","u2","u2","u2","u3","u3","u4","u4","u4"], "item": ["Mat","Strap","Bottle","Mat","Strap","Cookbook", "Blender","Cookbook","Mat","Strap","Bottle"], }) mat = pd.crosstab(tx["user"], tx["item"]) mat = (mat > 0).astype(int) # user-user cosine similarity, kept as a labelled DataFrame sim = pd.DataFrame(cosine_similarity(mat), index=mat.index, columns=mat.index) def neighbours(user, sim, k=3): s = sim[user].drop(user) # drop self-similarity (=1.0) return s.sort_values(ascending=False).head(k) print("Nearest neighbours of u1:") print(neighbours("u1", sim).round(2))
Drop the diagonal Every user is perfectly similar to themselves (cosine 1.0), so always remove self-similarity before ranking neighbours. Notice u4 comes back as u1's closest neighbour - identical baskets (Mat, Strap, Bottle) give cosine 1.0.
Demo 2 of 2

recommend_useruser: top-N unseen items ★ 11 min · build your own

Now the payoff. Score every candidate item by summing the similarities of the k neighbours who bought it, drop what the target already has, and return the top-N. This is exactly the math behind the b3 live recommender's "User-user CF" button.

Take the target's k nearest neighbours (from Demo 1).

For each item, sum the similarities of those neighbours who bought it - the weighted vote.

Remove items the target already owns.

Sort by score and return the top-N.

★ Run it now (Python) - weighted-vote recommenderimport numpy as np def recommend_useruser(user, mat, sim, k=3, n=3): # k nearest neighbours (exclude self) nbrs = sim[user].drop(user).sort_values(ascending=False).head(k) # weighted vote: sum neighbour similarity per item they bought scores = pd.Series(0.0, index=mat.columns) for other, s in nbrs.items(): scores += s * mat.loc[other] # closer neighbours count more # never recommend what the user already has owned = mat.columns[mat.loc[user] > 0] scores = scores.drop(owned) return scores.sort_values(ascending=False).head(n) print("Recommendations for u1 (user-user CF):") print(recommend_useruser("u1", mat, sim).round(2)) # Cookbook rises - u2, a close neighbour, bought it and u1 has not

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · In user-user CF, what becomes a recommendation for the target?

Find the target's nearest neighbours, take the items they have that the target lacks, and rank them by a weighted vote where closer neighbours count for more.

2 · Why did Amazon (Linden, Smith & York, 2003) move from user-user to item-item CF?

Users are numerous and change constantly, so their similarities go stale fast; item-item relationships are stable, so Amazon could precompute them and serve fresh recs to hundreds of millions of users.

3 · Why is cosine (not Pearson) the natural similarity for Kirana's data?

Pearson subtracts each user's mean to cancel generous vs harsh raters - useful for explicit stars. For binary purchase vectors, cosine on the pattern is the standard choice.
