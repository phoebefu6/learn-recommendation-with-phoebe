E-commerce vs fintech: same math, very different rules

A recommender is the same idea whether it sits inside a shopping app or a bank: predict what a customer will want next. But the two worlds could not be more different in what they optimize for, how much signal they have, and what happens when a recommendation is wrong. This session sets the two side by side - Amazon-style discovery and cross-sell against the bank's next-best-product - so you know which playbook you are actually running before you fund it.

Part 0

Why the same engine behaves differently

The four families from session 2 - collaborative, content-based, matrix factorization, deep - are just math. The math does not care whether it is ranking phone cases or savings accounts. What changes everything is the business context around the math: how big the catalog is, how much behaviour you can observe, and above all what it costs when the recommendation is wrong. In e-commerce a bad rec is an ignored suggestion. In fintech a bad rec can be mis-selling, a compliance breach, and a fine. Same engine, opposite guardrails.

Part 1 · e-commerce vs fintech at a glance

Five dimensions, two very different answers 7 min live

Before any tooling decision, put the two worlds on the same table. Every row below flips the design brief - the same recommender team would build two different things.

LiveThe e-commerce world: discovery at scale3 min▶

In a shop, the recommender's job is to help you find more of what you might want and to grow the basket:

  • Goal: discovery, cross-sell ("customers also bought"), and upsell to a better version.
  • Catalog: enormous - millions of items no human could browse - so the engine is doing real work no shelf layout could.
  • Signal: abundant and honest - every click, cart, and purchase is a data point, so cold-start is mostly a new-visitor problem, not a whole-business problem.
  • Cost of a wrong rec: low. You scroll past it. The engine optimizes for click-through rate, conversion, and basket size, and can afford to experiment.
Real world

Alibaba's Deep Interest Network (DIN). Zhou et al. (2018, KDD) published DIN - a deep click-through-rate model that learns which of your past behaviours matter for the item in front of you right now - and it was deployed across Alibaba's e-commerce platforms (the AliExpress / Lazada family). It is a real, named production algorithm, and it exists precisely because e-commerce has the ocean of behavioural signal and the low cost-of-error that let a team push accuracy hard. We cite the paper honestly; we are not reproducing Alibaba's live stack.

LiveThe fintech world: the right product, provably3 min▶

In a bank, wealth app, or insurer, the recommender's job flips from "discover more" to "offer the one right thing you are actually eligible and suited for":

  • Goal: next-best-product (a card, a loan, a fund) or next-best-action (a nudge, a review, a top-up) - a very short list of options.
  • Catalog: tiny - a handful of products - so ranking is easy; being allowed to offer is the hard part.
  • Signal: sparse. Customers hold few products and buy rarely, so cold-start is the normal state, not the edge case.
  • Cost of a wrong rec: high. Recommending an unsuitable product is mis-selling - a regulatory breach with real fines and customer harm. Suitability, explainability, and an audit trail are mandatory, and it is not financial advice.
Say the honest line out loud A fintech recommender surfaces products a customer is eligible and suited for - it does not give personalized investment advice. Behavioural data also implies consent and privacy duties (GDPR / PDPA). Bake that into the brief on day one, not after legal review.
Part 2 · the two funnels

Where the offer actually happens 5 min live

Draw the customer journey for each and one extra step appears on the fintech side that has no equivalent in the shop: a suitability gate the candidate must pass before it is ever shown.

Self-studyWhy the gate cannot be an afterthought2 min read▶

It is tempting to build the ranking model first and "add compliance later". In fintech that ordering fails, because the gate changes what the model is even allowed to rank:

  • Eligibility and suitability come first. A high-scoring product that the customer is not suited for must never surface - so the filter runs before ranking, not after.
  • Every offer needs a reason. Regulators and customers can ask "why was I shown this?" - the system must be able to answer, which pushes you toward explainable models and logged features.
  • The audit trail is a feature, not overhead. Who was offered what, when, and on what basis - retained and reviewable. In e-commerce nobody audits why you saw a phone case.
Real world

The e-commerce team can A/B its way forward; the fintech team cannot experiment on unsuitable offers. That single constraint is why fintech recommenders lean on fewer, more explainable models with hard business rules on top - not because the data scientists there are less capable, but because the cost of a wrong recommendation is on a completely different scale.

Walk-through 1 of 2

Read an e-commerce rec like a leader ◐ 7 min · think it through

No code today - a thought exercise. Picture the "customers who bought this also bought" rail on a marketplace and reason about what it is optimizing.

Name the goal: is this rail trying to help you find something new (discovery), add a companion item (cross-sell), or trade up (upsell)?

Name the signal: what behaviour fed it - your clicks, your cart, other shoppers' purchases? Notice how much signal is available.

Name the metric: the team is almost certainly watching click-through rate, conversion, and basket size - and can safely A/B test variants.

Name the cost of a miss: essentially zero. That freedom is why deep models like DIN live here.

Leader takeaway On the e-commerce side your questions are about lift and guardrails against filter bubbles and popularity bias - not about whether you were allowed to show the item.
Walk-through 2 of 2

Pressure-test a fintech next-best-product ◐ 8 min · think it through

Now picture a banking app suggesting a credit card or a fund. Same reasoning, but the questions change shape.

Name the goal: next-best-product or next-best-action from a short, fixed list - not open-ended discovery.

Find the gate: what eligibility and suitability checks must pass before this offer is allowed to appear at all?

Demand the reason: could the team explain, to a regulator or the customer, why this product was surfaced? If not, it should not ship.

Check the trail: is the offer, its basis, and its outcome logged for audit? Is consent for the behavioural data in place (GDPR / PDPA)?

Real world

Same team, opposite defaults. A leader who moves from a retail app to a bank keeps the same recommender talent but must re-wire the defaults: suitability before ranking, explainability over raw accuracy, audit over speed, and a standing reminder that this is product surfacing, not financial advice.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What most changes the design brief between e-commerce and fintech recommenders?

The math is the same. Catalog, signal, cost of error, and regulation flip the brief - the cost-of-error row reshapes the whole fintech build.

2 · In fintech, where must the suitability gate sit?

An unsuitable product must never surface, so the gate runs before ranking. It is mandatory, explainable, and audited - and the whole thing is product surfacing, not financial advice.

3 · Why can e-commerce teams push aggressive deep models like DIN more freely?

DIN (Alibaba, 2018) is deployed in e-commerce precisely because there is an ocean of clicks and carts and an ignored rec costs almost nothing - so the team can A/B its way to accuracy.
