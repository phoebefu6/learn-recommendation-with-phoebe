The team and the pipeline: who does what, and the hand-off

A recommender is not one person's model - it is a relay. An analyst finds the patterns, a data scientist trains the model, an ML engineer puts it into production, engineering surfaces it in the app, and the whole team watches it in the wild. Each role hands the next a specific artifact. This session walks that relay in order, gives you a RACI grid you can drop on your own org, and shows exactly where the builder track (b2 through b10) picks up each hand-off.

Part 0

A recommender is a relay, not a hero project

The single most common way a recommendation project stalls is treating it as "the data scientist's model". In reality the value only lands when five roles hand work cleanly down a chain - and a break at any hand-off kills the outcome. A brilliant model no one productionizes is a notebook. A served model no one surfaces in the app is invisible. This session gives you the map of the relay so you can staff it, sequence it, and spot the missing link before it costs a quarter.

Part 1 · the pipeline as a relay

Five roles, five hand-offs 7 min live

Read this left to right. Each box is a role; the chip under it is the artifact it hands to the next role. The whole track (b2-b10) is just this relay, done in code.

LiveThe relay, role by role4 min▶
  • Data analyst -> rules + patterns. Runs basket analysis on historical orders - which items sell together, with what support, confidence, and lift. This is the first, cheapest signal and it often ships as simple "bought together" rules before any model exists. (Builder track: b2.)
  • Data scientist -> trained model. Takes the patterns and the interactions matrix and trains and selects a model - collaborative filtering, content-based, matrix factorization, or deep - choosing the one that earns its complexity against the baseline. (b4-b7.)
  • ML engineer / DevOps -> served endpoint. Productionizes the chosen model: serving, latency budgets, batch vs real-time, a feature store so training and serving see the same features. This is where a notebook becomes a service. (b9.)
  • Engineering / front-end -> rec rails in the app. Revamps the UI to actually surface the recommendations - "because you bought", ranked rails, the right slots on the right screens. A great model in an ugly slot converts nothing. (b9.)
  • Everyone, led by DS + analytics -> dashboards + alerts. Monitors the algorithm in the wild - click-through, drift, when to retrain, and the A/B tests that prove it beats what came before. (b10.)
Real world

The order is a dependency chain, not a preference. Teams that let the data scientist run ahead of a serving plan end up with a model that scores beautifully offline and never ships. The healthiest recommender teams staff the ML-engineering and front-end hand-offs from day one, so "how will this be served and surfaced?" is answered before the model is even trained.

Part 2 · who is responsible for what

The RACI grid 5 min live

Same five tasks, mapped to who does and owns each one. R/A means that role does the work and owns the outcome; C means they are consulted. Use it to check your own team has an owner for every row.

Self-studyReading the grid, and finding your gap2 min read▶

The grid is a diagnostic. Two failure patterns show up instantly when you fill it in for your own team:

  • A row with no R/A. Nobody owns serving, or nobody owns the UI. This is where most projects quietly die - the model exists but never reaches a customer.
  • One role holding every R/A. The data scientist is doing analysis, training, serving, and the front-end. That is not a team, it is a bus-factor of one, and it will not scale past a demo.
Monitoring is everyone's job, led by someone The last row is deliberately shared - analysts, data scientists, and engineers all read the dashboards - but it still needs a single accountable owner (usually DS + analytics) or "everyone watches" becomes "no one watches". Name that owner.

The builder track maps straight onto these rows: b2 is basket analysis, b4-b7 are model training, b9 is serving and the UI revamp, and b10 is monitoring. When your practitioners are in the builder sessions, they are literally doing the columns of this grid.

Walk-through · think it through

Follow one recommendation from log to dashboard ◐ 8 min · no code

A thought exercise: trace a single "customers who bought this also bought" rec through all five hands, naming who holds it at each step.

Analyst. Notices in the order history that yoga mats and resistance bands sell together with high lift. Hands off a "bought together" rule.

Data scientist. Turns that and thousands of other co-purchases into a trained model that ranks items per user, and confirms it beats the popularity baseline. Hands off the model.

ML engineer / DevOps. Wraps the model in an endpoint that returns recommendations fast enough for a page load, backed by a feature store. Hands off the served endpoint.

Engineering. Adds a "because you bought your yoga mat" rail to the product page in the right slot. Hands off the live rec rail.

The team. Watches click-through and conversion on that rail, catches drift when the range changes for the season, and decides when to retrain. Hands off the dashboard - and the loop begins again.

Real world

Every hand-off is a place to lose the plot. The rule can be right and the model wrong; the model can be right and too slow to serve; the endpoint can be perfect and the rail buried three scrolls down; the rail can convert and no one is watching when it starts to drift. Leaders add value by protecting the hand-offs, not by owning any single box.

Homework

Map your own org - this week ◐ 20-30 min total

  • Draw the five-role relay for your team. For each box, write the name of the person who plays that role today.
  • Fill in the RACI grid: put an R/A in one cell per row, and Cs where a role is consulted.
  • Find the gap: which row has no owner, or which person is carrying more than two R/A boxes? That is your risk.
  • Write one sentence on the hand-off you are most worried about - model to serving, or serving to UI - and what would de-risk it.
  • Optional: share the filled grid with your practitioners heading into the builder track so leaders and builders are working from the same map.
