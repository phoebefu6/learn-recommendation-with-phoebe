Part 1 · industry monitoring practice

Monitoring: watch the numbers that pay the bills 5 min live

Offline metrics from b8 told you the model was good at launch. Monitoring tells you it still is. You track two layers: business outcomes (CTR, conversion, revenue per session) and model quality (precision@k on fresh, held-out interactions) - side by side, every day.

LiveThe two layers you watch every day3 min▶
  • Business metrics: click-through rate on the rail, add-to-cart and conversion, revenue per session. These are what leadership funds - dashboard them daily with alert thresholds.
  • Model-quality metrics: precision@k and recall@k computed on fresh interactions the model has not seen. A model can hold its offline score yet lose live CTR - watch both, because they diverge.
  • Alerting, not just charting: a dashboard nobody opens catches nothing. Set a threshold (a rolling CTR floor) and page someone when it breaks.
Real world

Fresh data is the honesty check. Precision@k on last month's test set flatters a stale model. Recomputing it on this week's clicks is what reveals decay - the same held-out logic from b8, run continuously instead of once. When live CTR and offline precision drift apart, trust the live number.

Part 2 · drift, feedback loops, guardrails

Drift, the feedback trap, and the guardrails 6 min live

Two things break a live recommender: the world changes underneath it, and the recommender changes the world. Name them precisely so you can detect and contain each.

LiveData drift vs concept drift - and how to detect them3 min▶
  • Data drift: the inputs shift - new products, a seasonal catalog, a different traffic source. The relationship still holds, but the feature distribution moved. Detect by comparing this period's distribution to a reference (a population-stability index or a chi-square style check).
  • Concept drift: user taste itself changes - what "relevant" means moves. Same inputs, different right answer. Detect it in the outcome metrics: precision@k and CTR sag even though inputs look stable.
  • The rule of thumb: inputs moved is data drift; the target moved is concept drift. Both call for retraining, but concept drift often needs new features or a new label definition, not just fresh data.
Self-studyThe feedback loop danger, and the guardrails2 min read▶

Here is the trap that makes recsys different from ordinary ML: the model trains on data its own recommendations generated. If it recommends popular items, those get clicked more, so next training they look even more popular - a rich-get-richer spiral into popularity bias and filter bubbles. Left alone, variety collapses and discovery dies.

  • Diversity injection: deliberately mix in less-popular but relevant items so the catalog keeps breathing.
  • Exploration: show a fraction of untested items to gather unbiased signal (the explore-vs-exploit trade).
  • Business rules: hard constraints - never recommend out-of-stock, respect suitability and consent, cap any single category's share of a rail.
Accuracy is not the only goal A recommender optimized purely for next-click CTR will happily build a filter bubble. Guardrails - diversity, exploration, business rules - are how you keep it useful, fair, and honest, not just accurate. This is the ethics rail from a5/a6 made operational.
Self-studyRetraining cadence + the A/B gate2 min read▶

How often to retrain is a cost-versus-freshness call: nightly or weekly for fast catalogs, monthly for stable ones, or event-triggered when a drift alert fires. But cadence only decides when you try - not what ships. Every change passes the same gate:

Offline gate: the candidate must beat the current model on b8 metrics (precision@k, NDCG) on held-out data. Fail here and it never reaches users.

Online A/B test: split live traffic, measure the primary business metric (conversion, revenue per session) on the real rail.

Ship rule: promote only if the primary metric wins and the guardrails hold (diversity did not collapse, no rule violated). Otherwise roll back.

Demo 1 of 2

A rolling-CTR monitor that alerts ★ 11 min · everyone builds

From a served-and-clicked log, compute a rolling daily CTR and flag the moment it drops below your threshold - the first line of defence against a silent decay.

Take an event log of impressions served and clicks received, one row per day.

Compute daily CTR, then a rolling 3-day average to smooth the noise.

Compare the latest rolling value to a threshold and raise an alert when it breaks.

★ Run it now (Python) - rolling CTR monitor + alertimport pandas as pd # Kirana rec-rail log: impressions served and clicks, per day log = pd.DataFrame({ "day": ["d1","d2","d3","d4","d5","d6","d7"], "served": [1000, 1000, 1050, 1000, 1100, 1000, 1050], "clicked": [ 42, 41, 42, 38, 37, 30, 27 ], }) log["ctr"] = log["clicked"] / log["served"] log["ctr_roll3"] = log["ctr"].rolling(3).mean() # smooth daily noise THRESHOLD = 0.030 # 3.0% rolling CTR floor latest = log["ctr_roll3"].iloc[-1] print(log[["day", "ctr", "ctr_roll3"]].round(4)) if latest < THRESHOLD: print(f"ALERT: rolling CTR {latest:.2%} is below floor {THRESHOLD:.1%}") else: print(f"OK: rolling CTR {latest:.2%} healthy")
Smooth before you alert Raw daily CTR is noisy - a single quiet day should not page anyone. Rolling the metric over a few days catches a real trend while ignoring the wobble, so your alerts mean something. Same idea scales to conversion and revenue per session.
Demo 2 of 2

A drift detector on the recommended-item mix ★ 11 min · build your own

CTR tells you that something dropped; a drift check helps tell you why. Compare the distribution of recommended items this week against last week with a population-stability index, and alert when the shift is large.

Count how often each item was recommended last week vs this week, and turn the counts into proportions.

Compute the population-stability index (PSI) across the item categories.

Apply the standard bands: below 0.1 stable, 0.1 to 0.25 moderate, above 0.25 significant drift - alert.

★ Run it now (Python) - PSI drift detectorimport numpy as np, pandas as pd # how often each item was recommended, last week vs this week last = pd.Series({"Yoga Mat": 400, "Protein": 300, "Dumbbells": 200, "Blender": 60, "Cookbook": 40}) this = pd.Series({"Yoga Mat": 640, "Protein": 300, "Dumbbells": 120, "Blender": 25, "Cookbook": 15}) # popularity creeping in eps = 1e-6 p = (last / last.sum()).clip(lower=eps) # reference distribution q = (this / this.sum()).clip(lower=eps) # current distribution # Population Stability Index: sum( (q - p) * ln(q / p) ) psi = float(((q - p) * np.log(q / p)).sum()) print(f"PSI = {psi:.3f}") if psi > 0.25: print("ALERT: significant drift - recommended-item mix shifted. Investigate.") elif psi > 0.10: print("WATCH: moderate drift.") else: print("OK: distribution stable.")
Real world

This is the feedback loop caught in the act. The mix tilting hard toward "Yoga Mat" is exactly the popularity spiral from Part 2 - the model over-recommending its own winners. A rising PSI on the recommended-item distribution is an early warning that variety is collapsing, often before CTR fully reacts. That is your cue to check the diversity guardrail and consider retraining.

2 · Why is the feedback loop uniquely dangerous in recsys?

3 · A retrained model beats the current one on offline precision@k. Does it ship?
