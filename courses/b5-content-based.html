Content-based filtering: recommend from features, not crowds

Collaborative filtering leans on the crowd - it needs other people's interactions before it can say anything. Content-based filtering leans on the item itself: its tags, its text, its features. Turn each Kirana item into a vector, average the vectors of what a shopper liked into a taste profile, and rank everything by cosine to that profile. Its superpower is cold-start for brand-new items; its trap is the filter bubble.

Part 0

Where this sits

In b3 and b4 you built collaborative filtering - item-to-item and user-to-user - which learn purely from the interaction matrix. Powerful, but blind on day one: a brand-new item nobody has touched has no column of neighbours, so CF can never surface it. Content-based filtering solves exactly that gap. It reads what an item is (its description, its tags, its features), so it can recommend an item with zero interactions - as long as it has features. Tonight you turn Kirana's catalog into TF-IDF vectors, build a per-shopper taste profile, and match the two with cosine similarity.

Part 1 · covers the recsys textbook content-based chapter

An item is a vector of features 6 min live

Content-based filtering never looks at who bought what. It looks at the item's own content - its description text, its category, its tags - and turns that into a numeric vector. Two items are similar if their vectors point the same way. A shopper is just the average of the items they liked.

LiveWhy TF-IDF, and a nod to embeddings3 min▶

You need to turn text into numbers. The classic choice is TF-IDF (term frequency x inverse document frequency):

  • Term frequency - how often a word appears in this item's text. "protein" in a protein-powder listing scores high.
  • Inverse document frequency - words common across the whole catalog (like "the", or "product") get down-weighted; distinctive words get up-weighted. TF-IDF automatically finds the words that characterize an item.
  • The result - each item is a sparse vector over the vocabulary, and similar items point the same direction.

Modern systems often swap TF-IDF for embeddings - dense vectors from a language model (sentence-transformers) that capture meaning, so "trainers" and "running shoes" land close even with no shared words. The pipeline is identical: text -> vector -> cosine. TF-IDF is the transparent, dependency-light place to learn it; embeddings are the drop-in upgrade.

Real world

Cold-start is where content-based earns its keep. A marketplace adds a hundred new listings a day. Collaborative filtering is silent on every one of them until enough people interact. Content-based can recommend a brand-new item the moment its description is written - so most production stacks run content-based as the cold-start layer and hand off to collaborative filtering once interactions accumulate.

Self-studyThe taste profile is just an average2 min read▶

Once each item is a vector, a user profile needs no new machinery. Take the items a shopper bought, and average their vectors. The mean is a point in the same feature space that leans toward whatever the shopper keeps buying - heavy on "fitness" if they buy fitness gear, heavy on "kitchen" if they buy appliances. Then score every item in the catalog by cosine similarity to that mean and recommend the top-N they do not already own. That is the whole algorithm.

Because the profile lives in feature space, you can read it: a taste profile weighted toward "nutrition" and "fitness" tells you, in plain words, what this shopper is into - unlike matrix factorization's latent factors in b6, which are powerful but opaque.

Part 2 · covers cold-start vs the filter bubble

The superpower and the trap 5 min live

Every family has one thing it is great at and one thing it is bad at. Content-based is great at new items and terrible at surprise. Know both before you ship it.

LiveThe filter bubble, and how to fight it3 min▶

The flip side of "recommend items like what you liked" is that the shopper is walled into their own history. Buy three yoga items and content-based will show you a fourth, and a fifth - never the cookbook that a like-minded shopper loved. That narrowness is the filter bubble, and it is the defining weakness of content-based filtering.

  • Blend it. Run content-based for cold-start coverage, collaborative filtering for taste discovery, and mix the two - a hybrid, which you meet in b8.
  • Inject diversity. Do not just take the top-N by cosine; re-rank to include some items that are relevant but different, so the shopper sees range.
  • Watch the loop. Recommenders shape behaviour - narrow recs beget narrow purchases beget narrower recs. You monitor exactly this feedback loop in b10.
Content-based is a starting layer, not the whole system It is the cheapest way to cover new items and new-ish users, and it is fully explainable. But shipped alone it gets boring fast. Almost every production recommender uses it as one signal in a blend, never as the only one.
Demo 1 of 2

TF-IDF item profiles, then "items like this" ★ 12 min · everyone builds

Vectorize Kirana's catalog with TfidfVectorizer, take the pairwise cosine_similarity, and ask for the items most similar to a given item - the "you might also like" strip that needs no interaction data at all.

Build a small item catalog: id, name, and a text field of description plus tags (fitness / kitchen / nutrition, mirroring our live catalog).

Fit TfidfVectorizer on the text column to get an items x terms matrix.

Compute the items x items cosine_similarity once.

For any item, sort its similarity row and return the top-N others.

★ Run it now (Python) - TF-IDF + cosine, items like thisimport pandas as pd from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer from sklearn.metrics.pairwise import cosine_similarity # Kirana item catalog: id, name, text (description + tags) items = pd.DataFrame({ "item": ["Yoga Mat","Dumbbells","Protein Powder", "Blender","Cookbook","Running Shoes"], "text": [ "fitness yoga mat non-slip home workout exercise", "fitness dumbbells weights strength home gym workout", "nutrition protein powder whey fitness recovery shake", "kitchen blender smoothie nutrition shake appliance", "kitchen cookbook healthy nutrition recipes meals", "fitness running shoes cardio exercise outdoor sport", ], }) vec = TfidfVectorizer() X = vec.fit_transform(items["text"]) # items x terms, TF-IDF weighted sim = cosine_similarity(X) # items x items similarity def similar_to(name, n=3): i = items.index[items["item"] == name][0] order = [j for j in sim[i].argsort()[::-1] if j != i][:n] return items["item"].iloc[order].tolist() print("Similar to Yoga Mat:", similar_to("Yoga Mat")) print("Similar to Blender: ", similar_to("Blender"))
Real world

Notice there are no users anywhere in that code. The similarity between Yoga Mat and Dumbbells comes entirely from shared words - "fitness", "home", "workout". That is why a listing added five minutes ago can already appear in a "similar items" strip: content-based reads the description, not the sales history.

Build a taste profile, recommend top-N ★ 10 min · build your own

Personalize it. Average the TF-IDF rows of the items a shopper bought into one taste-profile vector, then score every catalog item by cosine to that profile and return the top-N they do not already own.

Pick a shopper and the items they bought.

Average those items' TF-IDF rows into a single taste-profile vector.

Cosine-score the profile against every item, drop what they own, take the top-N.

Compare against a simpler binary tag-vector profile - same idea, no TF-IDF.

★ Run it now (Python) - taste profile from TF-IDF rowsimport numpy as np def recommend_for(bought, n=3): idx = items.index[items["item"].isin(bought)] profile = np.asarray(X[idx].mean(axis=0)) # mean of liked item vectors scores = cosine_similarity(profile, X)[0] # taste vs every item ranked = scores.argsort()[::-1] return [items["item"].iloc[j] for j in ranked if items["item"].iloc[j] not in bought][:n] # u1 buys fitness gear -> expect more fitness/nutrition, not kitchen print(recommend_for(["Yoga Mat","Dumbbells"]))

The same idea with a transparent, hand-built tag vector - no TF-IDF, just a binary bag of tags - makes the mechanics obvious:

★ Run it now (Python) - simpler binary tag-vector versiontags = pd.DataFrame({ "fitness": [1,1,1,0,0,1], "kitchen": [0,0,0,1,1,0], "nutrition": [0,0,1,1,1,0], }, index=items["item"]) def recommend_tags(bought, n=3): profile = tags.loc[bought].mean(axis=0).values.reshape(1, -1) scores = cosine_similarity(profile, tags.values)[0] ranked = scores.argsort()[::-1] return [tags.index[j] for j in ranked if tags.index[j] not in bought][:n] print(recommend_tags(["Yoga Mat","Dumbbells"])) # leans fitness/nutrition
Averaging is a choice, not a law The mean treats every past purchase equally. Real systems weight recent buys higher, decay old ones, and sometimes keep separate profiles per interest so a shopper who buys both gym gear and cookware does not get a muddy "average" of the two. That multi-interest idea is exactly what Alibaba's MIND tackles in b7.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What does content-based filtering use to make recommendations?

Content-based reads what the item is. Each item becomes a vector (e.g. TF-IDF of its text); similar vectors mean similar items. No interaction data required.

2 · Why does content-based filtering beat collaborative filtering on a brand-new item?

Collaborative filtering needs a column of interactions to find neighbours; a new item's column is empty. Content-based works from features the moment the item is listed - the cold-start win.

3 · What is the main weakness of content-based filtering?

Because it recommends items like what you already liked, it walls shoppers into their own history. Blend it with collaborative filtering and inject diversity to fight the filter bubble.
